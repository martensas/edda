\bookStart{Introduction to Galders}

“Galder” (Old Norse \emph{galdr}, Old English \emph{gealdor}, PGmc. \emph{*galdrą}) is the Old Germanic term for a chanted spell or charm.  The word is a verbal noun derived from the verb “gale” (ON \emph{gala}, Old English \emph{gala}, PGmc.~\emph{*galaną}), meaning ‘to sing, to chant’.  A galder is, then, that which is chanted or sung, more specifically a magical chant, whether for the purpose of healing or cursing.

The present section encompasses a number of such \inx[C]{galder}[galders] attested across the Old Germanic languages and composed in alliterative meters.  Not every text included may be a galder proper according to the definition of the term in the culture, but they share a common intent as magical charms, and many formulae and stylistic features reoccur throughout.

Note that this section will only include alliterative charms containing probable pre-Christian traditional elements, e.g., the invocation of the Heathen Gods or the use of old poetic formulae.  Syncretic texts which mix traditional and Christian elements (e.g., SJy 41 and most of the OE galders) are also included.  Thoroughly Christian prayers, however, even if composed in alliterative meters, are excluded from this section.  A few may instead be found under “Poetry on Christian Subjects”.

\bookStart{Old English Galders}

There survive twelve mediaeval Anglo-Saxon metrical charms, although it is questionable whether they all qualify as galders.  Notably for these texts is that they are preserved as the main text in manuscripts rather than marginalia, and that they have substantial ritual instructions alongside the words of the galders themselves.

The first four, \textlink{NineHerbs}, \textlink{WidDweorh}, \textlink{WidFerstice}, and “Childbirth”, are from the text known as the \emph{Lâcnunga} (\Lacnunga) in the manuscript Harley MS 585 (ca.~1000 \ce, siglum \LacnungaMS).  One galder from \Lacnunga, “For Loss of Cattle” (A), on fol.~180v--181r, is excluded as it is fully Christian without clear traditional elements.

The following two, “For Theft of Cattle” and \textlink{WidYmbe}, both belong to Corpus Christi College, Cambridge MS 41 (CCCC 41).  Two other galders in CCCC 41, “For Loss of Cattle” (B), a variant of (A) same-named galder found in \Lacnunga, and “A Journey Charm” are thoroughly Christian and therefore excluded.

The last three galders, “Against Wen”, “Against Water-Elf-Disease”, and \textlink{Acerbot}, are from various sources.  These will be discussed in the Introduction to each individual text.
