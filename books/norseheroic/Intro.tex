\section{The Walsing-Cycle}

The following poems belong to the Walsing-cycle, which is by far the best attested overarching Norse heroic narrative.  Since the poems themselves are rather difficult and allusive (not to mention incomplete due to the loss of the end of \textlink{Sigrdrifumal}, the beginning of \textlink{Brot}, and a large amount of material inbetween), there is certainly value in providing an overview of the Cycle in a single place.  This overview will not include the events told in \HelgakvidaOne--\HelgakvidaTwo, since these events do not connect to the broader narrative.

\subsection{Summary}

The narrative of the Cycle is rather convoluted and has clearly assimilated several originally unrelated narratives, but in the form it takes in the prose and later poetry of \Regius\ it goes as follows:

There was once an ettin called Rethmar.  He had three sons: Otter, Fathomer, and Rein.  Otter could take the shape of an otter and Fathomer that of a wyrm or serpent; Rein was a dwarf in stature and a talented smith.  One day, the Gods were fishing in Rethmar’s river when they killed the shape-shifting Otter.  Unwise of his true nature, they showed his flayed skin to his father, who seized them and demanded that they cover the skin with gold as a weregild.  To procure the money, the god Lock extorted the dwarf Andware for his treasure, including the ring Andwaresgift.  In anger, the dwarf cursed the stolen hoard to be the bane of all who possessed it.

The first killing soon followed.  One of Rethmar’s sons, Fathomer, grew greedy of his father’s new-found wealth; he slew him and took the gold to Gnit Heath, where he lay upon it in the shape of a wyrm.  In his dying moments, Rethmar appealed to his daughter Lingheath to avenge him by killing Fathomer.  She refused, but Rethmar told her to bear a daughter; she would, in turn, beget a son who could take the wealth.

Walsing was a great king.  He begot a son called Syemund, who went on adventures with his son Sinfittle.  After Sinfittle was poisoned by his step-mother Burhild due to a blood-feud, Syemund left her and went to Frankland, where he married Hardise, the daughter of King Eanlime (and, presumably, Lingheath).  Syemund and King Eanlime died in battle against the sons of Hunding, and not long thereafter Hardise bore Syemund’s son, Siward.  Hardise married Atholf, and Siward was raised by Atholf’s father, King Helpric.

One day Rein arrived at Helpric’s.  He took the young Siward and fostered him, told him about his own parentage and the theft of Rethmar’s hoard.  Rein forges the sword Gram for Siward and incites him to avenge his father and grandfather by killing the sons of Hunding; Siward does so.  After Siward has thus proved himself, Rein next incites him to kill Fathomer and take his gold.  The two go up to Gnit Heath where Fathomer lies, and Siward digs a trench where the wyrm is wont to go when going for water.  When Fathomer slithers over the trench, Siward thrusts his sword upwards and cuts him in half.  With his last breaths, Fathomer warns the reckless hero against taking the gold, but Siward does not listen.

At Rein’s request, Siward cuts the heart out of Fathomer and roasts it by the fire.  Rein has fallen asleep, and as the heart begins to froth, Siward thinks to feel with his finger whether it was hot enough.  As he does so, he burns himself, and puts his thumb in his mouth to cool it.  When the wyrm’s blood touches his tongue, he gains some of its power, and begins to understand the chattering of the small birds above him; they warn Siward that Rein is planning to betray him and take the gold for himself.  Eventually the lad grows convinced.  He kills Rein in his sleep and drinks his blood; then he eats Fathomer’s whole heart.  The birds give Siward yet more advice, telling him to load the gold onto his horse and then to ride up onto the Hindfell, where there lies a shield-maiden under a sleeping-curse.

Upon the Hindfell, Siward sees a radiating light.  By it he finds a circular shield-wall, and inside it there lies a warrior fast asleep under a helmet heavy byrnie.  He takes off the helmet and sees that it is a woman; he cuts the byrnie in two, waking her.  The woman gives her name as Syedrive and tells him that she had been stung with a sleeping-thorn by the god Weden after disobeying his orders.  As thanks for freeing her, Syedrive, who is also known as Byrnhild, blesses Siward and teaches him runic magic; the two exchange vows.  Although not stated explicitly, it seems that Siward then gives Byrnhild the ring Andwaresgift as a token.

In the middle of Syedrive’s advice, \Regius\ cuts off (as part of the Great Lacuna, see below).  For this part of the narrative, we therefore have to rely on allusions in the later poems of the collection along with \VolsungaSaga\ and \Skaldskaparmal\ 48--49.  After visiting Byrnhild, Siward rides to the court of the Yivickings, who are Guthrun and her brothers Guther and Hawn.  Their mother, Grimhild, gives Siward a potion to forget about Byrnhild and instead marry Guthrun; Siward, King Guther, and Hawn then swear oaths and become blood-brothers.  Grimhild convinces Guther to marry Byrnhild, and Siward, Guther, and Hawn ride away together to ask for her hand.  Byrnhild answers that she will only marry the man who will ride through a wall of fire for her.  Guther does not dare do this, and so Siward uses magic to switch shapes with him.  In the shape of Guther, Siward rides through the fire.  Byrnhild wishes to sleep with him immediately, but out of loyalty for his brother-in-law, Siward (still pretending to be Guther) instead places a sword between them in the bed, where they lay for three nights without touching each other.  Siward takes the ring Andwaresgift from Byrnhild and places another ring on her hand.  After this, the two couples wed on the same day, Siward with Guthrun, Guther with Byrnhild; they all live together at the stronghold of the Yivickings.

One day as Guthrun and Byrnhild are washing their hair, they argue over who has the best husband.  Byrnhild brags about how Guther rode through the fire for her, but Guthrun reveals that it was in fact Siward, not Guther, who did it; as proof, she takes forth the ring Andwaresgift, which Siward had previously taken from Byrnhild when they lay divided by the sword.  Byrnhild is heartbroken at the betrayal, since she had boasted only to marry the bravest and noblest man on the earth, whom she now knows to be Siward.  She decides that Siward and Guther cannot both be alive, but since Siward is married to Guthrun, it is he who must die.  She therefore incites Guther and Hawn to murder Siward, and it is here that \Regius\ resumes again.
Although Guther and Hawn are not happy about it, they yield to Byrnhild’s demands.  Since the two Yivickings are oath-sworn blood-brothers with Siward, they convince their younger brother, Godthorm, to do the deed.  He murders Siward in bed with Guthrun and dies in the process.  After this, Byrnhild sacrifices her handmaids and commits suttee in order to join Siward in Hell, the Underworld.  The Yivickings take Siward’s gold and become the possessors of Fathomer’s hoard; they hide it at the bottom of the Rhine.

Guthrun curses her brothers, but it is not yet her time to die.  Byrnhild’s brother, King Attle, blames the Yivickings for his sister’s death, and as a reconciliation Guther and Hawn marry Guthrun off to him.  The relationship between the two is uneasy, however.  Under the pretense of giving them wealth, Attle invites Guther and Hawn to his hall.  Guthrun attempts to send a warning through the ring Andwaresgift, enwrapped with a wolf-hair, but Guther does not heed it.  The two instead ride off to Attle where they are promptly captured.  Attle attemps to extort Guther to reveal the location of the hoard in exchange for the life of his brother Hawn, but Guther instead demands Hawn’s heart on a platter.  Hawn laughs as his heart is cut out of him.  Upon seeing the heart, Guther expresses his relief.  He is the only one who knows the location of the hoard, and he will not reveal it.  Enraged, Attle throws Guther in a snake-pit.  Guthrun smuggles a harp to her brother and he plays it for some time to soothe the serpents, but at last is killed.

Guthrun has now lost both her first husband and her brothers.  As revenge, she murders her own two sons with Attle and feeds them to him without letting him know; she reveals that he has been eating his own children.  King Attle drinks himself into a stupor and falls into bed; soon thereafter Guthrun stabs him to death in his sleep.  After this, she barricades the doors and sets fire to the hall, burning the retainers and shield-maidens inside.  Guthrun goes to the sea to drown herself, but her time has still not come.  Instead, she drifts over the waves to King Enacker.  With Enacker Guthrun begets two more sons, Sarle and Hamthew; at his court she also raises her daughter with Siward, Swanhild, and Enacker’s son from an earlier marriage, Earp.  When Swanhild is of age, she is married off to King Ermenric of the Gots.  Swanhild, however, falls victim to the cruel plans of Ermenric’s advisor Beck, who convinces Ermenric’s son Randwigh to rape her.  Beck then reveals the affair to Ermenric, who hangs his son and has horses trample Swanhild to death.

Guthrun learns of Swanhild’s death and has now lost everything in the world.  She incites her last surviving relatives, Sarle and Hamthew, to take revenge on their sister by killing King Ermenric; Sarle and Hamthew are to cut off the king’s hands and feet, but her step-son Earp should cut off his head.  The two brothers set out and encounter Earp on the road.  He offers them assistance “as a hand would help a hand or a foot a foot”, but they are annoyed with his way of speech and kill him.  At last the two reach the court of King Ermenric, where they manage to maim the king by cutting off his hands and feet.  They fail in decapitating him, however, and regret their unwarranted killing of their half-brother; with Earp’s help, says Hamthew, they would have cut off the head, not the limbs alone.  Ermenric calls out to his retainers to stone the two boys and they die.  At the same time, Guthrun herself commits suttee and declares her wish to join Siward in the afterlife.  With this, the cycle ends.

\subsection{Historical origins}

The Walsing-Cycle in its classical form has clearly assimilated a large number of originally unrelated (pseudo-)historical narratives into a single whole, tied into a cohesive narrative through (fabricated) genealogical ties and the common thread of the curse of Fathomer’s hoard.  That this has happened is clear enough when one considers the historical lifetimes of the historical people depicted in the poems.  For instance, King Ermenric (\emph{Hermanaricus}) of the Gots reigned from ca.~296--376 \ce, while King Attle (\emph{Attila}) of the Huns lived from ca.~406--453 \ce.

Some of the events depicted do, however, appear to have old kernels of truth.  The trampling of Swanhild and the revenge taken by her two brothers Hamthew and Sarle, for instance, is found in essentially the same form in Jordanes, \emph{Getica}, and is quite likely to be a genuine folk-memory (see Introduction to \textlink{Gudrunarhvot}).  The fall of the Burgends or Burgundians, led by King Guther (\emph{Gundaharius}), at the hands of Hunnish mercenaries, is likewise historical.

It is of note that the historical basis is lost more and more as the tradition progresses.  In the linguistically archaic \textlink{Atlakvida}, which depicts the fall of King Guther and Hawn at the hands of King Attle, the conflict is still portrayed as an ethnic conflict between the Gots (or Burgends) and the Huns.  In the younger \textlink{Atlamal} and in most of the other heroic poetry in this section, this remaining political awareness is totally lost, replaced by a fabricated familiar conflict.

\subsection{Other legendary sources}

Outside of \Regius, the poetry found in the present section is attested directly in three Norse sources: \VolsungaSaga, \Nornagest, and \Skaldskaparmal.  Outside of these three texts, there are various other sources, both Norse and more broadly Germanic, that attest the underlying narratives for the poems.

\subsubsection{The Saw of the Walsings}

The main Norse source for the Walsing-cycle is the \emph{Vǫlsunga saga} ‘Saw of the Walsings’ (\VolsungaSaga).  This text is a prosimetrum largely derived from the heroic half of \Regius.  \VolsungaSaga\ cites a total of 30 stanzas from four poems, and paraphrases many more.

Although much of \VolsungaSaga\ is directly paraphrased from the heroic poems of \Regius, its redactor did not merely cobble together the poetry that was in front of him.  Instead, he has made an attempt to combine the often conflicting and chronologically overlapping poems into a unified chronological prose narrative, sometimes rather creatively (e.g., in his handling of the contradictions between \textlink{Atlakvida} and \textlink{Atlamal}).  He also appears to have relied on texts never included in \Regius, especially for the lives of Syemund and Walsing, Siward’s father and grandfather, respectively.  To a lesser extent, there appears to be influence from Low German epic sources.

\subsubsection{The Matter of Scaldship}

\emph{Skáld-skapar mál} (\Skaldskaparmal) is the second book of the Prose Edda.  In order to explain a number of kennings, chapters 46--49 summarise the entire Walsing-cycle and cite two stanzas from \textlink{Fafnismal}.

\subsubsection{The Strand of Norn-Guest}

\emph{Norna-Gests þáttr} (\Nornagest) is a short prose narrative embedded in the larger saw of King Anlaf Trueson.  In it, the eponymous Norn-Guest, tells various legends of which he supposedly has first-hand experience.  Of relevance to us are its summaries of and substantial citations from the poems \textlink{Reginsmal} and \textlink{Helreid}.

\subsubsection{German material}

Niebelungenlied and other german stories.

\subsubsection{Old English sources}

The earliest story for the wyrm-slaying narrative is in fact found in the early 8th c.~OE poem \Beowulf, ll.~875--900.  In this text it is not Siward, but instead his father Syemund, who slays the Wyrm and takes its treasure.  Syemund is called a Walsing (OE \emph{Wælsing}, 877) and “the son of Walse” (OE \emph{Wælses afará}, 897), which is clearly a more original name for the Norse Walsing (ON \emph{Vǫlsungr}, which is in fact a back-formation from the clan name \emph{Vǫlsungar} ‘Walsings’, who are originally the descendants of \emph{*Vǫlsi}).  Siward is not named, but Syemund is said to have gone on ettin-slaying adventures with his nephew Fittle (OE \emph{Fitulá}), who is equivalent to the Norse Sinfittle (ON \emph{Sin·fjǫtli}).

Although the attestation in \Beowulf\ is a story within a story and not very detailed, we are still able to glean that at least one strand of the Walsing-cycle narrative was important in Anglo-Saxon England around 700 \ce.  The poet’s bestowal of praise on Syemund, who is called the “most widely renowned of adventures among mankind for deeds of valour \dots” (898--900), indicates that the narrative had already acquired a unique significance.

\section{The poems}

The present section contains eighteen poems found in \Regius\ and six fragments found in \VolsungaMS.  Unlike the mythological poetry in \Regius, the order of its heroic poems follows a clear plan.

The first three poems (\textlink{HelgakvidaOne}, \textlink{HelgakvidaHjorvardssonar}, \textlink{HelgakvidaTwo}) deal chiefly with the heroes Hallow Harwardson and Hallow Hundingsbane, of whom the latter is believed to be a reincarnation of the former.  Hallow Hundingsbane is said to be the son of Syemund the Walsing and Burhild, which ties these poems to the rest of the Walsing-Cycle.

The following four poems (\textlink{Gripisspa}, \textlink{Reginsmal}, \textlink{Fafnismal}, \textlink{Sigrdrifumal}) narrate the youth and early successes of Siward Syemundson.  The latter three form a continuous narrative and are better read as a single prosimetrum than as three individual poems (cf.~Introduction to \textlink{Reginsmal}).
After \textlink{Sigrdrifumal}, there follows the so-called Great Lacuna (\textlink{Lacuna}); the poetry therein would, however, have dealt with Siward’s marriage to Guthrun and his betrayal of Byrnhild.

The next four poems (\textlink{Brot}, \textlink{GudrunOne}, \textlink{Sigurdskamma}, \textlink{Helreid}) deal with Siward’s death at the hands of his in-laws, the Yivickings, and the ramifications on his unwitting wife, Guthrun, and on Byrnhild.

The next five poems (\textlink{GudrunTwo}, \textlink{GudrunThree}, \textlink{Oddrunargratr}, \textlink{GudrunTwo}, \textlink{Atlakvida}, \textlink{Atlamal}) jump forwards in time to after Guthrun’s second marriage to King Attle.  The section culminates in Attle’s death at the hands of Guthrun.

The last two poems (\textlink{Gudrunarhvot}, \textlink{Hamdismal}) deal with Guthrun’s sons taking revenge for Swanhild, her only daughter with Siward.

\subsection{Short summaries}

\emph{I Hęlga kviða Hundings-bana} (‘\textbf{The First Lay of Hallow Hundingsbane}’, \textlink{HelgakvidaOne}) deals with the life of Hallow Hundingsbane, his romance with the walkirrie Syerun, and his vanquishing of his enemies of Hathbrod and Hunding.

\emph{Hęlga kviða Hjǫr·varðs sonar} (‘\textbf{The Lay of Hallow Harwardson}’, \textlink{HelgakvidaHjorvardssonar}) is a prosimetrum.  It jumps back in time and describes the life and death of Hallow Harwardson and his romance with the walkirrie Sweve (the earlier reincarnations of Hallow Hundingsbane and Syerun); this poem is the earliest in the Walsing-Cycle chronology.

\emph{II Hęlga kviða Hundings-bana} (‘\textbf{The Second Lay of Hallow Hundingsbane}’, \textlink{HelgakvidaTwo}) is a prosimetrum about the life of Hallow Hundingsbane and his romance with Syerun, beginning with his birth and ending with his return as a ghost in order to take farewell to Syerun.

\emph{Grípis spǫ́} (‘\textbf{The Spae of Griper}’, \textlink{Gripisspa}) tells of the fate of Syemund and Sinfittle; it then introduces Siward Syemundson and sees him hear a prophecy about his own life from his uncle Griper.

\emph{Ręgins mǫ́l} (‘\textbf{The Speeches of Rein}’, \textlink{Reginsmal}) is the first of a sequence of three prosimetra dealing with Siward’s life.  Siward takes instruction from the dwarven smith Rein, who tells him about the origin of Fathomer’s hoard; Siward then avenges his father and lethally wounds the wyrm Fathomer.

\emph{Fáfnis mǫ́l} (‘\textbf{The Speeches of Fathomer}’, \textlink{Fafnismal}) follows as Siward speaks with the dying Fathomer, who warns him against taking his treasure; Siward does so anyways.  The hero inadvertently gets a taste of Fathomer’s heart-blood and learns the speech of birds; they warn him that Rein is plotting against him.  Siward kills him, takes the hoard, and follows the birds to the sleeping walkirrie Syedrive.

\emph{Sigr·drífu mǫ́l} (‘\textbf{The Speeches of Syedrive}’, \textlink{Sigrdrifumal}) sees Siward free the sleeping Syedrive or Byrnhild; she teaches him runic magic and gives him important advice.

The text cuts off with the \emph{Great Lacuna}; I have therefore inserted seven “\textbf{Fragments from the Walsing-Cycle}” (\textlink{Lacuna}).  This poetry deals with Siward’s disguise as Guther, his wooing of Byrnhild, and Guthrun’s revealing of the deception to Byrnhild.

\emph{Brot af Sig·urðar kviða} (‘\textbf{The Fragment of a Lay of Siward}’, \textlink{Brot}) continues the narrative and describes how Byrnhild instigates her husband, Guther, to murder Siward; Byrnhild then chastises Guther for his betrayal of his brother-in-law and describes Siward’s loyalty towards him.

\emph{I Guð·rúnar kviða} (‘\textbf{The First Lay of Guthrun}’, \textlink{GudrunOne}) describes the impact of Siward’s death on his wife, Guthrun, as three noble ladies try to make her weep and release her pent-up sorrows; the third one shows her Siward’s body; Guthrun at last weeps and laments his death.  She curses her brother Guther and Siward’s wife Byrnhild.  Byrnhild blames her brother Attle and says that she will not live long; fires burn from her eyes when she looks at Siward’s body.

\emph{Sig·urðar kviða in skamma} (‘\textbf{The Short Lay of Siward}’, \textlink{Sigurdskamma}) tells how Byrnhild incited Guther and Hawn to murder Siward and how they got their younger brother, Godthorm, to do the deed.  The majority of the poem deals with Byrnhild in the aftermath of the killing; sacrifices a number of her handmaids and servants and then commits suttee by disembowelment in order to follow Siward on the pyre; as she lies dying, Byrnhild foresees Guthrun’s tragic future.

\emph{Hęl-ręið Bryn·hildar} (‘\textbf{The Hell-ride of Byrnhild}’, \textlink{Helreid}) follows Byrnhild after she has been burned on the pyre; on the way through the Underworld she encounters a troll-woman who upbraids her for her cruelty; Byrnhild responds by explaining her actions.

\emph{II Guð·rúnar kviða} (‘\textbf{The Second Lay of Guthrun}’, \textlink{GudrunTwo}) is a dirge told by Guthrun after she has been married off to Attle.  She recounts the loss of her first husband, Siward, and her second marriage to Attle.  The poem concludes as Guthrun relates Attle’s prophetic dreams about how she will murders him and her sons.

\emph{III Guð·rúnar kviða} (‘\textbf{The Third Lay of Guthrun}’, \textlink{GudrunThree}) sees Guthrun accused of adultery by Attle’s maidservant, Herch.  Guthrun is proven innocent and Herch is drowned.

\emph{Odd·rúnar grátr} (‘\textbf{The Weeping of Ordrun}’, \textlink{Oddrunargratr}) describes how Ordrun, Guther’s lover and Attle’s sister, helps a young woman named Burgny give birth; after this, Ordrun relates her tragic love for Guther and her inability to prevent his death at the hands of Attle.

\emph{Atla kviða} (‘\textbf{The Lay of Attle}’, \textlink{Atlakvida}) tells how King Attle invites Guther and Hawn to his court under the guise of giving them treasure; instead, he betrays and murders them by cutting the heart from Hawn and putting Guther in the snake-pit.  Guthrun avenges her brothers by murdering their two sons and feeding them to Attle; she then burns him and his host alive in the hall.

\emph{Atla mǫ́l in grǿn-lęndsku} (‘\textbf{The Greenlandish Speeches of Attle}’, \textlink{Atlamal}) tells the same narrative as \textlink{Atlakvida} in an elaborated and younger form; in this poem, the wives of Guther and Hawn have prophetic dreams warning them, and although Guthrun kills Attle, she does not burn the hall.

\emph{Guð·rúnar hvǫt} (‘\textbf{The Instigation of Guthrun}’, \textlink{Gudrunarhvot}) takes place later on.  After Guthrun murdered Attle and her sons, she tried to drown herself; instead she was carried by the waves to King Enacker, where she raised her daughter by Siward, Swanhild, and begot two new sons.  Swanhild was married off to King Ermenric, who had her trampled to death by horses after she was seduced by his son.  Guthrun incites her two sons to avenge their sister; they set out to do so.  Guthrun prepares a pyre for herself and makes a speech; she will at last commit suttee and follow Siward.

\emph{Ham·ðis mǫ́l} (‘\textbf{The Speeches of Hamthew}’, \textlink{Hamdismal}) depicts the same events as \textlink{Gudrunarhvot}, but follows the sons of Guthrun and Enacker as they set out to murder King Ermenric and avenge their sister.  On the way the two boys meet their half-brother by Enacker; he offers to help them, but they kill him instead.  They get to Ermenric and cut off his hands and feet, but the king survives long enough to call out to his troops to stone the lads; they regret the killing of their half-brother before they die.
