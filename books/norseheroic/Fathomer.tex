\section{Introduction}

The \textbf{Speeches of Fathomer} (\Fafnismal) is a heroic prosimetrum dealing with the aftermath of Siward’s slaying of the wyrm Fathomer.

\subsection{Textual preservation and transmission}

\Fafnismal\ is only preserved in full in \Regius, where it has the title \emph{Frá dauða Fáfnis} ‘From the death of Fathomer’.  It forms a continuous prosmetric narrative with the preceding \textlink{Reginsmal} and the following \textlink{Sigrdrifumal} and should not be read in isolation.

The poetry of \Fafnismal\ is closely paraphrased by \VolsungaSaga\ 18--20, and it is clear that the now-lost source underlying that text was near-identical to \Regius.

Furthermore, three stanzas are cited in \SnorraEdda: st.~13 in \Gylfaginning\ and 32--33 in \Skaldskaparmal.

\subsection{Origin and motifs}

Siward’s slaying of the wyrm Fathomer was an exceptionally famous story in Scandinavia during the Wiking Age and Middle Ages.  Outside of \textlink{Reginsmal}--\Fafnismal\ and \VolsungaSaga, the narrative is referenced in Scaldic poetry (TODO) and retold in \Skaldskaparmal.

The killing is also depicted pictorally on numerous objects.  The most important of these depictions is the Swedish runic inscription Sö 101 from Ramsund, Södermanland, dating to ca.~1030 \ce\ (Fig. \ref{fig:ramsund}, p.~\pageref{fig:ramsund}).  Sö 101 is inscribed on a single large, flat rock-face with a generic memorial epitaph, but the runes themselves are placed in the body of a long serpent or wyrm, and the rock-face is ornamented with numerous details from the story.  At the bottom right of the carved surface, a figure thrusts a sword through the wyrm’s body, and in the space enclosed by the wyrm several important events following Siward’s slaying of Fathomer are depicted with remarkable fidelity to the version preserved in the Norse-Icelandic sources.

\subsection{Summary}

The poem begins just as Siward has fatally wounded Fathomer.  The wyrm notes his young age asks him for his name and father; he exclaims that the sword has pierced his heart (1).  The prose says that Siward hid his name out of a superstitious belief in names as curses (P1).  Siward tells Fathomer that his name is Noble Deer and that he has no father (2).  Fathomer asks how he was conceived, in that case (3).  Siward now reveals his name and paternity; he is Siward, son of Syemund, but Fathomer will not know of his lineage (4).

Fathomer asks who incited Siward to murder him; he notes that Siward must have had a peculiar father to slay him at such an age (5).  Siward responds that he was only driven by his courage and has no accomplices beyond his own hands; one who is soft in childhood will seldom harden by age (6).  Fathomer tells Siward that he is not as brave as he thinks and calls him a captive of war (7).  Siward responds that he is not a captive; Fathomer himself has experienced that he is loose and free (8).  Fathomer berates him for his spiteful answers and tells him that his gold be his death (9).  Siward answers that every man must die at some point; he will take the gold regardless (10).  Fathomer warns him further about the curse; he will perish soon, in any sort of unforeseen circumstance (11).

The following four stanzas are mythological and rather out-of-place.  Siward asks about the origins of the Norns and Fathomer answers (12--13); he then asks about where the Gods will fight against Surt (14--15).

Fathomer reflects about his own arrogance; while he lay on his hoard he carried a “helmet of awe” above mankind and thought himseslf superior to all (16).  Siward answers that no man is ever invincible (17).  Fathomer says that he blew venom while he lay on his father’s treasure (18); Siward says that he did, but that the helmet which he wore only created greater enmity for him (19).

The wyrm once again tries warning the hero about the curse; he advices him to ride home immediately (20).  Siward tells him that he has heard the counsel, but that he will take the treasure anyways while Fathomer expires in his blood (21).  Fathomer speaks his last words: Rein betrayed him, but he will betray Siward too; he dies (22).

A second prose passage says that Rein had disappeared while Siward slew Fathomer, but that he returned as Siward was drying the blood from his sword (P2).  Rein greets Siward and praises his bravery (23).  Siward tells him that bravery is never certain until proven (24).  Rein notes that the youth is happy, but reflects that he himself played a part in the death of his own brother (25).  Siward tells him that it was indeed only with Rein’s help that he could have slain Fathomer (26).

The prose tells us that Rein went up to Fathomer and cut the heart from him and drank some of his blood (P3).  Rein asks Siward to roast Fathomer’s heart by the fire while he himself sleeps (27).  Siward, perhaps unhappy with the assignment, tells him that it was he used his strength against the Wyrm while Rein lay hiding (28).  Rein tells him that it was only by the use of the sword which he gave Siward that he could have slain him (29), but Siward tells him that courage is better than the strength of the sword (30--31).

One of the most famous parts of the narrative comes in prose.  Siward took Fathomer’s heart and roasted it, but to check whether it was done he touched it with his finger; he burned himself and put the finger in his mouth.  At the moment the blood touched his tongue he understood the speeches of some birds above him, and one of them was chattering (P4).
The first bird chatters that it would think Siward wiser if he ate the heart himself (32).  The second continues that Rein is planning to betray him and avenge his brother (33).  The third says that Siward ought to kill Rein first and take the whole treasure for himself (34).  The fourth says that would think Siward smart if he would follow the counsel of its sisters (35).  The birds continue: Siward is not as wise as they thought if he chooses to spare Rein (36--37); he ought to kill him and take his wealth (38).  Siward says that evil fate will not overpower him; he will slay Rein (39).  So he does; he then eats Fathomer’s heart and drinks his blood and Rein’s (P5).

The birds continue with their advice.  They tell Siward to hurry to the house of a noble maiden (40).  She is Yivick’s daughter, and fate will guide him to her so that they may marry (41).  Next they tell him of a hall surrounded by fire on the Hindfell (42); the walkirrie sleeping there has been stung with a sleeping-thorn (43), and Siward can only break her sleep in accordance with fate (44).

The prose concludes.  Siward followed the advice of the birds and rode up to Fathomer’s dwelling where he took the gold and the helmet of awe.  He loaded the treasures onto his horse, Grane (P5).

\bpg\bpa Sig·urðr reið eptir slóð Fáfnis til bǿlis hans ok fann þat opit ok hurðir af járni ok gę́tti; af járni vóru ok allir timbr-stokkar í húsi’nu en grafit í jǫrð niðr.
Þar fann Sig·urðr stór-mikit gull ok fylldi þar tvę́r kistur.
Þar tók hann ǿgis-hjálm ok \edtrans{gull-brynju}{golden byrnie}{\Bfootnote{An obviously fictional object, the weight of which would be far too great to be practical.  Golden byrnies do not otherwise commonly feature in the Norse heroic material; the only other attestation of the present compound in the Norse corpus is in \textlink{Sigurdskamma}[47]/3.}} ok sverð’it Hrotta ok marga dýr-gripi ok klyfjaði þar með Grana.
En hestr’inn vildi eigi fram ganga fyrr en Sig·urðr steig á bak hǫ́num.\epa

\bpb%
Siward rode along Fathomer’s trail to his dwelling and found it open and doors and rabbets of iron. Of iron were also all the timber trunks in the house, and dug down into the earth. There Siward found very much gold and he filled there two chests. Then he took the helmet of awe and a golden byrnie and the sword Rotte and many precious things and loaded Grane with them. But the horse did not want to go forth until Siward mounted his back.\epb\epg
