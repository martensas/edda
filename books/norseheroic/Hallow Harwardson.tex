\section{Introduction}

The \textbf{Lay of Hallow Harwardson} (\HelgakvidaHjorvardssonar) is a heroic prosimetrum and the second of the three Hallow-poems.

\subsection{Textual preservation and transmission}

\HelgakvidaHjorvardssonar\ is found only in \Regius, on foll.~22r/4--24r/12, where it follows \textlink{HelgakvidaOne} and is followed by \textlink{HelgakvidaTwo}.

As has been shown by \textcite[151--153]{Lindblad1980}, \HelgakvidaHjorvardssonar\ forms a particularly close orthographic group with its fellow prosimetrum and neighbour, \textlink{HelgakvidaTwo}.  In fact, the two texts are so closely related orthographically that they appear to derive from a single source, which must have preceded the compilation of \Regius.

\subsection{Meter and stanza}

Like the following \textlink{HelgakvidaTwo}, but unlike the preceding \textlink{HelgakvidaOne}, \HelgakvidaHjorvardssonar\ is a prosimetrum that mixes prose with poetry in \Fornyrdislag\ and \Ljodahattr.

The text consists of 10 prose passages and 43 stanzas.
24 stanzas (1--11, 31--43) are in \Fornyrdislag, and 18 stanzas (13--30) are in \Ljodahattr.  A single stanza (12) mixes meters, with its ll.~1--2 in \Fornyrdislag\ and 3--4 in \Ljodahattr.

The most common line-length for the \Fornyrdislag\ stanzas is 4 (21/24: 1--4, 6--11, 31, 34--43).  Other represented line lengths are 5 (5), 3 (32), and 6 (33).

Of the 18 pure \Ljodahattr\ stanzas, the archetypal configuration \emph{abc abc} is represented in 16/18 stanzas (13--25, 27, 29--30).  St.~26 has \emph{abc abcc abc} and 28 has \emph{abc abcccc}.

The mixed st.~12 is of the type \emph{[abab] abc}, where the bracketed lines are in \Fornyrdislag.

\subsection{Frá Hjǫr·varði ok Sigr·linn (‘From Harward and Syelind’)}

\bpg\bpa\mssnote{\Regius~22r/4}%
Hjǫr·varðr hét konungr; hann átti fjórar konur.  Ein hét Alf·hildr; sonr þeira hét Heðinn.  Ǫnnur hét Sę́r·eiðr; þeira sonr hét Humlungr.  In þriðja hét Sin·rjóð; þeira sonr hét Hymlingr.  Hjǫr·varðr konungr hafði þess heit strengt at eiga þá konu er hann vissi vę́nsta.  Hann spurði at Sváfnir konungr átti dóttur \edtext{*allra}{\Afootnote{\emph{‘vęnallra’} \Regius}} fegrsta; sú hét Sigr·linn.  Ið·mundr hét jarl hans; Atli var hans sonr, er fór at biðja Sigr·linnar til handa konungi.  Hann dvalði⸗sk vetr-langt með Sváfni konungi.  Frán·marr hét þar jarl, fóstri Sigr·linnar; dóttir hans hét Á·lǫf.  Jarl’inn réð, at meyjar var synjat, ok fór jarl’inn heim.  Atli jarls sonr stóð einn dag við lund nokkurn, en fugl sat í limu’num uppi yfir hánum, ok hafði heyrt til, at hans menn kǫlluðu vę́nstar konur þę́r, er Hjǫr·varðr konungr átti.  Fugl’inn kvakaði, en Atli hlýddi hvat hann sagði. Hann kvað:\epa

\bpb Harward was the name of a king; he had four wives.  One was called Elfhild; their son was called Headen.  The other was called Searhad; their son was called Humbling.  The third was called Sindread; their son was called Himbling.  King Harward had undertaken a boast to have the woman whom he knew to be the most handsome.  He learned that King Swebner had a daughter fairest of all; she was called Syelind.  Idmund was the name of his earl; Attle was his son, who journeyed to ask for Syelind’s hand on behalf of the king.  He stayed over the winter with King Swebner.  Frenmar was the name of an earl there, the foster-father of Syelind; his daughter was called Anlave.  The earl decided that the maiden was denied him, and the earl journeyed home.  Attle, the earl’s son, one day stood in a certain grove, but a bird sat in the branches above him and had overheard that his men said that the women which King Harward had were the most handsome.
The bird twittered, and Attle listened to what he said.  Quoth he:\epb\epg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22r/16}%
\speakernote{Fugl kvað:}%
\llap{„}\alst{S}átt þú \alst{S}igr·linn, \hld\ \alst{S}váfnis dóttur, &
\alst{m}ęyna fęgrstu \hld\ i̇́ \alst{m}unar-hęimi? &
Þó \alst{h}ag⸗ligar \hld\ \alst{H}jǫr·varðs konur &
\alst{g}umnum þykkja \hld\ at \alst{G}lasis-lundi.“\eva

\bvb\speakernoteb{Quoth the bird:}%
“Hast thou seen Syelind, Swebner’s daughter, \\
the fairest of maidens in the realm of love \ken{world}, \\
even as Harward’s wives seem pleasant \\
to the men in Glazerslund?”\evb\evg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22r/18}%
\speakernote{Atli kvað:}%
\llap{„}Munt við \alst{A}tla \hld\ \alst{I}ð·mundar son &
\alst{f}ugl \alst{f}róð-hugaðr \hld\ \alst{f}lęira mę́la?“ &
\speakernote{Fugl kvað:}%
\llap{„}Mun’k ef mik \alst{b}uðlungr \hld\ \alst{b}lóta vildi &
ok \alst{k}ýs’k þat’s ek vil \hld\ ór \alst{k}onungs garði.“\eva

\bvb\speakernoteb{Quoth Attle:}%
“Wilt thou with Attle Idmund’s son, \\
O wise-minded bird, speak yet more?” \\
\speakernoteb{Quoth the bird:}%
“I will, if the chieftain would make a bloot for me \\
and I may choose what I wish from the house of the king.”\evb\evg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22r/20}%
\speakernote{Atli kvað:}%
\llap{„}Kjós⸗at-tu \alst{H}jǫr·varð \hld\ né \alst{h}ans sonu &
né inar \alst{f}ǫgru \hld\ \alst{f}ylkis brúðir, &
ęigi \alst{b}rúðir \hld\ þę́r’s \alst{b}uðlungr á; &
kaupum \alst{v}el saman, \hld\ þat ’s \alst{v}ina kynni.“\eva

\bvb\speakernoteb{Quoth Attle:}%
“Choose not Harward nor his sons, \\
nor the fair brides of the marshaller--- \\
not the brides which the chieftain has! \\
Let us deal well together; that is the manner of friends.”\evb\evg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22r/22}%
\speakernote{Fugl kvað:}%
\llap{„}\alst{H}of mun’k kjósa, \hld\ \alst{h}ǫrga marga, &
\edtrans{\alst{g}oll-hyrnðar kýr}{golden-horned kine}{\Bfootnote{Formulaic, also found in \textlink{Thrymskvida}[23]/1.}} \hld\ frȧ \alst{g}rams búi, &
ef hǫ̇num \alst{S}igr·linn \hld\ \alst{s}øfr ȧ armi &
ok \alst{ȯ}·nauðig \hld\ \alst{jǫ}fri fylgir.“\eva

\bvb\speakernoteb{Quoth the bird:}%
“Hoves will I choose, many harrows, \\
golden-horned kine from the ruler’s estate, \\
if Syelind will sleep on his arm \\
and without coercion follow the lord.”\evb\evg


\bpg\bpa\mssnote{\Regius~22r/24}%
Þetta var áðr Atli fǿri. En er hann kom heim ok konungr spurði hann tíðinda, hann kvað:\epa

\bpb This was before Attle journeyed.  But when he came home and the king asked him for the tidings, then quoth he:\epb\epg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22r/25}%
\llap{„}\edtrans{Hǫfum \alst{ę}rfi\emph{ði} \hld\ ok ękki \alst{ø}rendi}{We have a hardship and no errand}{\Bfootnote{I.e., ‘we have had great struggle and got no results.’  A formulaic word-pair; cf.~\textlink{Thrymskvida}[10]/1, 11/1.}}; &
\alst{m}ara þraut ȯra \hld\ ȧ \alst{m}ęgin-fjalli, &
urðum \alst{s}íðan \hld\ \alst{S}ę́·morn vaða; &
þȧ vas oss \alst{s}ynjat \hld\ \alst{S}váfnis dóttur, &
\alst{h}ringum gǿddrar, \hld\ es vér \alst{h}afa vildum.“\eva

\bvb “We have a hardship and no errand. \\
Our steeds expired on the mighty fell; \\
thereafter we had to wade through Seamorn. \\
Then we were refused Swebner’s daughter, \\
endowed with rings, whom we wished to have.”\evb\evg


\bpg\bpa\mssnote{\Regius~22r/28}%
Konungr bað at þeir skyldu fara annat sinn; fór hann sjálfr. En er þeir kómu upp á fjall ok sá á Sváfa-land lands-bruna ok jó-reyki stóra, reið konungr af fjalli’nu fram í land’it ok tók nátt-ból við á eina. Atli helt vǫrð ok fór yfir á’na. Hann fann eitt hús. Fugl mikill sat á húsi’nu ok gę́tti ok var sofnaðr. Atli skaut spjóti fugl’inn til bana en í húsi’nu fann hann Sigr·linn konungs dóttur ok Á·lǫfu jarls dóttur ok hafði þę́r báðar braut með sér.  Frán·marr jarl hafði hamat⸗sk í arnar líki ok varit þę́r fyr her’num með fjǫl-kynngi.
Hróð·marr hét konungr, biðill Sigr·linnar. Hann drap Sváfa-konung ok hafði rę́nt ok brennt land’it. Hjǫr·varðr konungr fekk Sigr·linnar en Atli Á·lǫfar.\epa

\bpb The king bade that they should journey another time; he journeyed himself.  But when they came up onto the fell and beheld upon Swebeland the singed land and the great dust-clouds from the horses, the king rode down from the fell forth into the land and took his night-lodgings by a river.  Attle kept watch and journeyed over the river; he found a lone house.  A great bird sat upon the house guarding it, and it had fallen asleep.  Attle shot the bird to its death with a spear, but within the house he found Syelind, the king’s daughter, and Anlave, the earl’s daughter; and he took them both away with him.  Earl Frenmar had shifted hames into the likeness of an eagle and had guarded them against the host with manicunningness.
Rothmar was the name of a king, the suitor of Syelind.  He had slain the king of the Swebes and had plundered and burned the land.  King Harward got Syelind, but Attle got Anlave.\epb\epg


\bpg\bpa\mssnote{\Regius~22v/5}%
Hjǫr·varðr ok Sigr·linn áttu son mikinn ok vę́nan. Hann var þǫgull; ekki nafn festi⸗sk við hann. Hann sat á haugi. Hann sá ríða val-kyrjur níu ok var ein gǫfug⸗ligust. Hón kvað:\epa

\bpb Harward and Syelind begot a son, large and handsome.  He was silent; no name stuck to him.  He sat on a mound.  He saw nine walkirries riding, and one was the most worshipful.  Quoth she:\epb\epg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22v/7}%
\llap{„}Síð munt, \edtrans{\alst{H}ęlgi}{Hallow}{\Bfootnote{The walkirrie here gives the silent boy his name, \emph{Hęlgi} ‘the Holy One’.}}, \hld\ \alst{h}ringum ráða, &
\alst{r}íkr \alst{r}óg-apaldr, \hld\ né \alst{R}ǫðuls-vǫllum, &
---\alst{ǫ}rn gól \alst{á}r⸗la--- \hld\ ef þú \alst{ę́} þęgir, &
þó’tt \alst{h}arðan \alst{h}ug, \hld\ \alst{h}ilmir, gjaldir.“\eva

\bvb “Late wilt thou, Hallow, rule the rings \\
---O strong strife-apple-tree \ken{warrior}---or the Raddlewolds \\
---the eagle crowed in early morn---if thou art for ever silent, \\
even if thou hast a hard heart, O prince!”\evb\evg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22v/9}%
\Ballnote{It was customary to give a gift to a child along with their name as part of the so-called \emph{nafn-fęsti} ‘name-fastening’ (cf. \emph{Origo Gentis Langobardorum} 1:12 n., in the Appendix to \textlink{Grimnismal}).  Since the walkirrie, Sweve, has just given Hallow his name, he asks her for such a gift; he will take none other than her hand.}%
\speakernote{[Hęlgi] kvað:}%
\llap{„}\alst{H}vat lę́tr fylgja \hld\ \alst{H}ęlga nafni, &
\alst{b}rúðr \alst{b}jart-lituð, \hld\ alls \alst{b}jóða rę́ðr? &
Hygg fyr \alst{ǫ}llum \hld\ \alst{a}t·kvę́ðum vel; &
\alst{þ}igg ęigi \alst{þ}at \hld\ nema \alst{þ}ik hafa!“\eva

\bvb\speakernoteb{Quoth [Hallow]:}%
“What lettest thou follow Hallow’s name \\
---O bright-faced bride---since thou dost offer it? \\
Think well ahead before all decisions; \\
I will accept nothing other than to have \emph{thee}!”\evb\evg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22v/11}%
\Ballnote{Instead of her hand, Sweve gives Hallow the location of an ancient sword.}%
\speakernote{[Sváfa] kvað:}%
\llap{„}\alst{S}verð vęit’k liggja \hld\ i̇́ \alst{S}ig·ars-holmi, &
\alst{f}jórum \alst{f}ę́ra \hld\ an \alst{f}imm tǫgu; &
\alst{ęi}tt es þęira \hld\ \alst{ǫ}llum bętra &
\alst{v}íg-nesta bǫl \hld\ ok \alst{v}arit golli.\eva

\bvb\speakernoteb{Quoth [Sweve]:}%
“Swords I know lying in Sigarsholm: \\
four less than fifty. \\
One of them is better than all---\\
a \inx[C]{bale} of war-covers(?) \ken{shields}---and it is covered with gold.\evb\evg

\edtrans{\alst{H}ringr ’s i̇́ \alst{h}jalti}{A ring is on its hilt}{\Bfootnote{The sword is a so-called \emph{ring-sword}. ęn ȧ \edtrans{\alst{v}al-bǫstu}{walbast}{\Bfootnote{An unclear part of the sword-hilt; this word also occurs in \textlink{Sigrdrifumal}[6]. TODO: Falk Waffenkunde.}} \hld\ \alst{v}erpr naðr hala.“\eva

\bvb A ring is on its hilt; heart is in the middle; \\
terror is in the point for him who gets to own it. \\
Along the edge lies a serpent painted in blood, \\
but on the walbast an adder eats its tail.”\evb\evg


\bpg\bpa\mssnote{\Regius~22v/15}%
\Ballnote{The prose informs us of the identity of the walkirrie with whom Hallow has just conversed.}%
Ey·limi hét konungr; dóttir hans var Sváfa.  Hón var val-kyrja ok reið lopt ok lǫg.  Hón gaf Helga nafn þetta ok hlífði hánum opt síðan í orrustum.  Helgi kvað:\epa

\bpb Eanlime was the name of a king; his daughter was Sweve.  She was a walkirrie and rode over air and sea.  She gave Hallow this name and afterwards often shielded him in battles.  Quoth Hallow:\epb\epg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22v/17}%
\llap{„}Est⸗at, \alst{H}jǫr·varðr, \hld\ \alst{h}ęil-ráðr konungr, \lednopb &
\alst{f}olks odd-viti, \hld\ þó’tt \alst{f}rę́gr séir; &

\bvb “Thou art not, Harward, a king of wholesome counsel--- \\
O point-knower of the host \ken{warrior}---although thou be renowned. \\
Thou didst let fire ravage the settlements of chieftains, \\
but they did no injury against thee.\evb\evg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22v/19}%
Ęn \alst{H}róð·marr skal \hld\ \alst{h}ringum ráða, &
þęim es \alst{ǫ́}ttu \hld\ \alst{ȯ}rir niðjar; &
sá sé⸗sk \alst{f}ylkir \hld\ \alst{f}ę́st at lífi, &

\bvb But Rothmar shall rule the rings \\
which \emph{our} kinsmen have owned. \\
That marshaller fears the fewest things in life; \\
he thinks he will rule the inheritance of the fully dead.”\evb\evg


\bpg\bpa\mssnote{\Regius~22v/21}%
\Ballnote{This prose passage introduces a long section of the poem, namely the dialogue between the heroes Attle and Hallow and the ettin-woman Rimegird, culminating in the latter’s turning to stone at daybreak (sts.~12--30).}%
Hjǫr·varðr svaraði at hann myndi fá lið Helga ef hann vill hefna móður-fǫður síns.  Þá sótti Helgi sverð’it er Sváfa vísaði hánum til.  Þá fór \edtext{h\emph{an}n ok Atli}{\Afootnote{sens.~emend.; \emph{hón ok Atli} \Regius}} ok felldu Hróð·mar ok unnu mǫrg þrek-virki.  Hann drap Hata jǫtun er hann sat á bergi nokkuru.  Helgi ok Atli lágu skipum í Hata-firði.  Atli helt vǫrð inn fyrra hlut nę́tr’innar.  Hrím·gerðr Hata dóttir kvað:\epa

\bpb Harward answered that he would provide a troop for Hallow if he wished to avenge his mother’s father.  Then Hallow sought out the sword to which Sweve had shown him the way.  Then he and Attle journeyed and slew Rothmar and accomplished many great deeds of strength.  He slew the ettin Hate when he sat on a certain mountain.  Hallow and Attle lay in their ships in Hate’s firth; Attle kept watch during the first part of the night.  Quoth Rimegird, Hate’s daughter:\epb\epg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22v/27}%
\Ballnote{With the second half of this stanza, the meter shifts from \Fornyrdislag\ to \Ljodahattr.  This meter continues throughout the rest of the section (sts.~12--30).}%
\llap{„}\alst{H}vęrir ’ro \alst{h}ǫlðar \hld\ i̇́ \alst{H}ata-firði? &
\alst{Sk}jǫldum es tjaldat \hld\ ȧ \alst{sk}ipum yðrum, &
\alst{f}rǿkn⸗liga látið, \hld\ \alst{f}átt hygg yðr s\emph{éa}⸗sk; &
\ind \alst{k}ęnnið mér nafn \alst{k}onungs!“\eva

\bvb “Who are these heroes in Hate’s firth? \\
With shields it is canopied upon your ships; \\
ye bear yourselves bravely; I ween ye fear little. \\
\ind Teach me the name of the king!”\evb\evg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22v/29}%
\speakernote{Atli kvað:}%
\llap{„}\alst{H}ęlgi hann \alst{h}ęitir \hld\ ęn þú \alst{h}vęr⸗gi mátt &
\ind vinna \alst{g}rand \alst{g}rami, &
\alst{éa}rn-borgir \hld\ ’ro umb \alst{ǫ}ðlings flota &
\ind knegu-t oss \alst{f}ǫ́lur \alst{f}ara.“\eva

\bvb\speakernoteb{Quoth Attle:}%
“Hallow is he called, but thou canst in no wise \\
\ind accomplish harm against the chief. \\
Iron strongholds surround the athling’s floater; \\
\ind no shrews can destroy us.”\evb\evg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22v/31}%
\llap{„}\alst{H}vé þik \alst{h}ęitir,“ \hld[kvað Hrím·gęrðr] „\alst{h}alr inn ȧm-átt⸗ki? &
\ind Hvé þik \alst{k}alla \alst{k}onir? &
\alst{F}ylkir þér trúir \hld\ es þik i̇́ \alst{f}ǫgrum lę́tr &
\ind \alst{b}ęits stafni \alst{b}úa.“\eva

\bvb “What art thou called,” quoth Rimegird, “O uncanny man--- \\
\ind what does mankind name thee? \\
The marshaller trusts thee when he lets thee stay \\
\ind in the tacked ship’s prow.”\evb\evg


\bvg\bva\mssnote{\Regius~22v/33}%
\speakernote{[Atli kvað:]}%
\llap{„}\alst{A}tli ek hęiti, \hld\ \edtrans{\alst{a}tall}{terrible}{\Bfootnote{A pun, the name \emph{Atli} ‘Attle’ is identical to \emph{atli} ‘the terrible one’, weak masculine of the adj.~\emph{atall}.}} skal’k þér vera, &
\ind mjǫk em’k \alst{g}ífrum \alst{g}ramastr; &
\alst{ú}rgan stafn \hld\ ek hęfi \alst{o}pt búit &
\ind ok \alst{k}valðar \alst{k}veld-riður.\eva

\bvb\speakernoteb{[Quoth Attle:]}%
“Attle I am called; I shall be terrible toward thee; \\
\ind I am by far the cruelest to troll-hags. \\
In a drizzled prow I have often stayed \\
\ind and tormented evening-rideresses.\evb\evg

\bvb\speakernoteb{[Quoth Sweve:]}%
“What happened to Hallow, Harward’s son? \\
A cruel injury has found its way to me! \\
If the sea betrayed him or a sword bit him, \\
against that man I shall avenge the harm!”\evb\evg

the chieftain who was the best beneath the sun. \\

\bvb\speakernoteb{[Quoth Hallow:]}%
“Be hale, O Sweve!  Thou shalt share of my mind; \\
in the Home this will be our very last meeting. \\
On the chieftain the gashes bleed; \\
the sword has come nearest to my heart.\evb\evg

\bvb I bid thee, Sweve---O bride, do not weep \\
if thou wilt harken to my speech--- \\
that thou for Headen makest a bed \\
and showest the young warrior thy affections.”\evb\evg

\bvb\speakernoteb{[Quoth Sweve:]}%
