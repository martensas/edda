\section{Introduction}

The \textbf{Second Lay of Guthrun} (abbrev. \GudrunTwo) takes the form of a monologue told by Guthrun, who reflects on her tragic fate.  Within the framework of the heroic half of \Regius, this poem (and its prologue) is important since it shifts the focus away from the hero Siward (who is now dead) and instead deals with King Attle and his dealings with the three Yivickings: Guthrun and her brothers Guther and Hawn.  This focus persists for the following four heroic poems in the collection (\textlink{GudrunThree}, \textlink{Oddrunargratr}, \textlink{Atlakvida}, and \textlink{Atlamal}).

\subsection{Textual preservation and transmission}

\GudrunTwo\ is only found in full in \Regius, on foll.~TODO--TODO, where it follows \textlink{Helreid} and is followed by \textlink{GudrunThree}.

The poem was available to the redactor of \VolsungaSaga, who cites three stanzas (18/5--6, 21--22) and paraphrases many more (TODO).

\subsection{Summary}

The poem itself is introduced by the prose passage \emph{Dráp Niflunga} (‘The Slaying of the Nivlings’), which serves as a prologue.  This passage is given its own large initial with the same size as the poem itself, and may therefore also be interpreted as a distinct text from \GudrunTwo.

\subsection{Dráp Niflunga (‘The Slaying of the Nivlings’)}

\bpg\bpa%
\Ballnote{This orienting prose passage serves briefly to summarise the narrative context behind the following 5 poems (\textlink{GudrunTwo}--\textlink{Atlamal}), all of which deal with King Attle.}%
\edtext{Gunn·arr}{\Afootnote{The \emph{G} is an ornamented initial 3 lines in height in \Regius.}} ok Hǫgni tóku þá gull’it allt, Fáfnis arf. Ó·friðr var þá milli gjúkunga ok Atla; kenndi hann gjúkungum vǫld um and·lát Bryn·hildar. Þat var til sę́tta, at þeir skyldu gipta hánum Guð·rúnu, ok gǫ́fu henni ó·minnis-veig at drekka áðr hón játti at giptask Atla. Synir Atla vóru þeir Erpr ok Eitill, en Svan·hildr var Sig·urðar dóttir ok Guð·rúnar. Atli konungr bauð heim Gunn·ari ok Hǫgna, ok sendi \edtrans{Vinga eða \edtext{Kné·frøð*}{\Afootnote{gramm.~emend.; \emph{Kné·frøðr} (with erroneous abbreviation mark for nom.~sg.~\emph{-r}) \Regius}}}{Winge or Kneefrith}{\Bfootnote{The messenger is known as \emph{Vingi} in \textlink{Atlamal}, but \emph{Kné·frøðr} in \textlink{Atlakvida}.}}.  Guð·rún vissi vélar ok sendi með rúnum orð at þeir skyldu eigi koma ok til jar-tegna sendi hón Hǫgna hring’inn And·vara-naut ok knýtti í vargs-hár.  Gunn·arr hafði beðit Odd·rúnar, systur Atla, ok gat eigi; þá fekk hann Glaum·varar, en Hǫgni átti Kost·beru; þeira synir vóru þeir Sól·arr ok Snę́v·arr ok Gjúki.  En er gjúkungar kómu til Atla, þá bað Guð·rún sonu sína at þeir bę́ði gjúkungum lífs, en þeir vildu eigi.  Hjarta var skorit ór Hǫgna en Gunn·arr settr í orm-garð.  Hann sló hǫrpu ok svę́fði orma’na, en naðra stakk hann til lifrar.\epa

\bpb Guther and Hawn then took all the gold, Fathomer’s inheritance.  Then there was enmity between the Yivickings and Attle; he blamed the Yivickings for Byrnhild’s passing.  Such were their terms, that they would marry Guthrun off to him, and they gave her a forgetfulness-draught to drink before she agreed to be married off to Attle.  The sons of Attle were Earp and Oatle, but Swanhild was the daughter of Siward and Guthrun.  Attle invited Guther and Hawn to his home and sent Winge or \inx[P]{Kneefrith}.  Guthrun knew his wiles and sent words in runes that they should not come, and as a sign she sent Hawn the ring Andwaresgift and tied a wolf’s hair through it.  Guther had asked for the hand of Ordrun, Attle’s sister, and did not get her; then he got Gleamware, but Hawn had Costbearer to wife; their sons were Solwer and Snower and Yivick.  But when the Yivickings came to Attle, Guthrun bade her sons to plead for the life of the Yivickings, but they would not do so.  The heart was cut out of Hawn, but Guther was placed in the snake-pit.  He struck his harp and soothed the serpents, but an adder stung him unto the liver.\epb\epg
