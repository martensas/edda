\section{Preface}

What is this book?  It is a book of poems, really, of songs---songs dating to the first millennium \ce\ or some time thereafter.  These poems about the Gods and heroes of the Old Germanic peoples were sung or recited through the Dark Ages, the fall of Rome and the volcanic winter of 536, in ships and shores of England, in frozen settlements on Iceland and Greenland, in Danish fields, Swedish forests, and Norwegian firths, in English monasteries and the ear of King Charlemagne.

Compared to the epic traditions of Classical Antiquity, the Germanic heroic ballads do not tell of a very ancient past.  On the contrary, many of them concern events documented in contemporary Classical sources far more accurate than these illiterate ballad-singers working centuries after-the-fact could ever aspire to be.  But their goal was never historical accuracy.  For them these times were in the mythic past, and who none could faithfully recall them but the High One, the One-Eyed Himself.  They were bards and storytellers and prophetesses, keepers of an ancient poetic tradition far predating the subjects with which it dealt.  Theirs was a tradition infused with the Indo-European spirit of the unending steppes and the snowcapped mountains, the soaring eagle and the galloping steed.  In its stoic fatalism, its tales of great love and struggle, deprivation and human tragedy, heroic friendship and betrayal, their retellings of historical events were far older in spirit than the more factually correct mediaeval chroniclers which chronologically predated them.  In this way they were, in their often rough-hewn simplicity, as old as Homer, as old as the Vedas---or maybe older still.

The Northern epics are the songs which the West for many centuries forgot.  We forgot them when we became just that, \emph{West\-erners}, Romans, Christians.  Already in the Middle Ages this literature was spurned by the learned.   It was Alcuin, the English scholar, who in a 797 letter upbraided his less learnèd English brethren who would rather sing pagan Saxon songs about Ingeld than the Psalms of David---“what does Ingeld have to do with Christ?”, he asked.  Around the same time, it was Louis the Pious, king of the Franks, who burned his father Charlemagne’s great collection of Frankish epic poetry.  For these new men steeped in Roman learning, whose souls were circumcised, even if their bodies were not, the ethos of the old folk-songs seemed too warlike, too fatalistic, in a word, too \emph{pagan}.

The echoes of the same stories in time suffered a tragic fate in England and the Continent.  The heavily damaged copies, written in idioms and hands too outdated to be understood by later ages, were cut up and used for their thick leather as covers for younger, more modern books.  But measuring fate would not have it that they were, like so many other European pagan traditions, forever lost.  It was on Iceland, that northern island outpost on the edge of the known world, where the songs of Gods and heroes lived longest upon the lips of hoary upholders of tradition.  It was that most distant offshoot of Germandom, whose unyielding settlers sailed as far as Greenland and North America, which in time came to preserve what the center had lost.

The Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, the greatest surviving storehouse of the songs of the Germanic gods and heroes, was rediscovered in Iceland in 1642.  Together with the Prose Edda, it laid the ground for the new field of the study of Germanic antiquity, which over time developed in lockstep with the evolution of Western science and culture in general and that of philology and linguistics in particular.  Over the centuries it was joined by the rediscoveries of \emph{Beowulf}, the Exeter Book, \emph{Hildebrandslied}, the Merseburg charms, the Gallehus horns, and countless other minor sources.

No work could aim to give a complete overview of all scholarly efforts in this direction since the 17th century, and this is, in any case, not a book of intellectual history.  The goal of the present edition is only to provide a small selection of previous insights, together with some new ones, which (it is hoped) may inspire the curiosity of the English-speaking reader and increase his or her understanding and respect for these highly important, frequently highly difficult, texts.

\section{The historical and cultural context}

  I have mentioned Germanic peoples, but who are they?  It is a central thesis throughout this book that the Germanic-speaking nations---the English, Scandinavians, Dutch, Frisians, Germans, and others---were originally united by much more than their tongues alone; that these nations, although greatly splintered by the turns of history, ultimately share not only a common ancestral language, but a common culture, religion, and origin in an Old Germanic civilisation.

  \subsection{Language and culture}

  All Germanic languages---like the Romance languages from Latin---ulti\-mately descend from Proto-Germanic, spoken around the first centuries \ce.  Unlike its contemporary, Classical Latin, Proto-Germanic is a reconstructed language attested only in a few borrowed names and in short, fairly late runic inscriptions.  Still, we have enough data from various points to be able to reconstruct it confidently (as done, e.g., by \cite{Orel2003}, \cite{Kroonen2013}, \cite{Ringe2017}), and such reconstructions will appear throughout this book.

  The Proto-Germanic idiom, in turn, derives from the wholly unattested Proto-Indo-European, whence also spring the Albanian, Armenian, Baltic and Slavic, Celtic, Greek, Italic, and Indo-Iranian tongues, along with the now-extinct Anatolian, Tocharian, and a likely myriad other now-lost languages.  The Germanic branch most likely broke away from its closest Indo-European linguistic relatives in the early 3rd millennium \bce, and has developed on its own ever since, undergoing important changes in its sounds, word-hoard, word-order, and grammar.  Since Proto-Indo-European is the earliest reconstructable linguistic ancestor of all the Germanic languages, and since several of its branches are attested long before Germanic, it will at times also be referenced.

  Around the start of the first millennium \ce, the Germanic family itself broke apart into three branches: the Northern, Western, and Eastern.  Only the first two are still extant.  To the first belong the Scandinavian languages descended from Old Norse; to the second English, Frisian, Dutch, Low and High German (including Yiddish); and to the third the extinct Gothic and Vandalic.  It is generally held to be the case on the basis of a number of common innovations that Northern and Western branches form a single subclade, Northwest Germanic, to the exclusion of East Germanic; on the other hand, the unity of East Germanic has recently come into question due to the paucity of securely attested shared innovations \parencite{Kim2024}.

  The divergence of the Germanic branches coincides with the great movement of these peoples in the earlier part of first millennium \ce, and especially following the fall of Rome, during a period known as the Migration Period (ca.~300--600 \ce).  In the West, the Anglo-Saxons conquered much of Britain, while the Franks established France; in the south, the Longbeards (Lombards), Visigoths, Vandals conquered as far as northern Italy, Iberia, and Northern Africa; in the east, the Eastern Gots established their kingdom in present-day Ukraine.
  Since the Wielbark culture, which is almost certainly to be identified with the early ancestors of the Goths \parencite{McColl2024}, emigrated from Scandinavia around 50 \bce, this forms a useful reference point for the time of the splintering of the Germanic language family.

  The similarities of these peoples in antiquity went far beyond language.  During the Migration Period, there was something of a cultural flow between the whole Germanic-speaking sphere, especially those who were not yet Christianized.  This long-distance contact is seen both in archaeology (e.g., in shared animal art-styles, \cite{Salin1904}, or in the chiefly burial of a genetically Gothic man on the German shores of the North Sea, \cite{Myburgh2026}) and in later literary sources like \textlink{Widsith} \parencite{Neidorf2013} and \Nornagest, which portray Germanic-speaking characters as being able to travel far and wide throughout the Germanic kingdoms of the Migration Period.  The Got Jordanes (\emph{Getica} 24) briefly mentions how a Scandinavian king was able to take refuge with Thedric of the Gots, undoubtedly aided by a shared language.

  Nor were these affinities entirely lost on the migrating tribes themselves.  Both the Gots and the Longbeards still recalled their old homeland of Scandinavia and the names of the related peoples still living there (Jordanes, \emph{Getica} 10, 16--25; \emph{Origo Gentis Langbardorum} 1, edited under the appendix to \textlink{Grimnismal}); the Anglo-Saxons, too, never forgot that they had come from Northern Germany and Jutland \parencite{Gretzinger2022}, nor that they descended from the Angles and Saxons still living there \parencite[173--176]{Neidorf2013}.

  More broadly, numerous legal customs like the husband’s “morning-gift” to his wife on the day of their wedding, the ring-oath, and the twelve-man jury were spread far by these expanding tribes.  So too was their ancient two-stemmed naming system with parallels among other Indo-Europeans (cf., e.g., English Edwin < Old English \emph{Éad-wine} < Proto-Germanic \emph{*Auda-winiz} ‘wealth-friend’).

  As for explicitly religious customs, the worship of many Gods (the Eese, ON \emph{ę̇sir}, OE \emph{ėse}, Gothic \emph{Ansis} in Jordanes, \emph{Getica} 78, 79; cf.~\textlink{Hamdismal}[25]/1 n.) is attested throughout the historical and geographic range of the Germanic peoples, as are the use of sacred rings (the Gothic ‘holy’ ring of Pietroassa \parencites[280]{Antonsen2002}[173--174]{MacLeod2006}, cf.~Ambrose of Milan’s letter 10:9 which suggests the wearing of bracelets by Gothic priests \parencite[425]{Capelle2010}; \textlink{Havamal}[110], \textlink{Hildebrandslied} 32), animal sacrifices, and certain forms of ritual warfare (\textlink{Voluspa}[23] n.).
  Also widely attested is the worship of the gods Weden, Ing, and Thunder, the former of whom appears in His name Geat at the head of both the Gothic and Anglo-Saxon genealogies (\textlink{Hamdismal}[25]/1 n.).

  \subsection{Ancestry}

  It seems clear enough that there appears to have existed in the first millennium \ce\ a common Old Germanic culture with a shared language, culture, and religion, and that shared ballads and tales were able to propagate throughout the whole area \parencite{Neidorf2013}.  The question that remains to be answered is whether these peoples ultimately shared common descent, or whether they merely formed a common cultural horizon, but were ultimately unrelated; by the latter understanding, the mediæval accounts of Bede, Jordanes, the Longbeard Origin Myth, and other authors who claimed a common Northern origin for the Germanic peoples would be mistaken or even fabulous stories made up for political or social reasons.

  Fortunately, recent genetic advances have shown that the lines of linguistic expansion throughout human history generally line up with signals of genetic kinship, and have in effect confirmed the accounts of the mediaeval authors and, in turn, what earlier scholars of the 19th and early 20th centuries took for granted: in most cases, speakers of related languages ultimately descend, to a greater or lesser degree, from a single group of speakers of a proto-language.

  What the genetics show us is that the Germanic peoples originate in Northern Europe, chiefly Scandinavia and Northern Germany, and that the speakers of Proto-Germanic were genetically indistinguishable from modern native Scandinavians \parencites{McColl2024}{McColl2026}.  They in turn derived much of their ancestry from the early Proto-Indo-Europeans of the Pontic-Caspian steppe, who mixed with Early European Farmers to the west to form the Corded Ware Culture, which over time expanded in all directions \parencite{McColl2024}.

  In the west, this early Indo-European culture formed the Bell Beakers and later Celts who conquered Ireland and Iberia \parencite{McColl2026Celtic}; in the south, it formed the Italic peoples who eventually became the Romans; in the east, it formed the Sintashta culture, or Proto-Indo-Iranians, whose descendants became the conquering Iranians and Vedic Indians who founded the Zoroastrian and Vedic religions; in the northeast, it formed the Balts and Slavs; and in Scandinavia, it formed the Battle Axe and Single Grave cultures which eventually gave way to the Nordic Bronze Age, the ancestors of the Germanic peoples.

  In all cases, including Scandinavia, the expansion of the Indo-Europeans was that of a warlike and patriarchal culture not unlike its later descendants, whether Roman or Germanic.  Indeed, present-day native Scandinavians, who are largely the direct biological descendants of the Nordic Bronze Age, carry almost no ancestry from the Stone Age Scandinavian Hunter Gatherers that preceded them \parencite{Allentoft2024}.

  Interestingly, it may be added that although the genes for light eyes and hair colour were present already on the Pontic-Caspian steppe, it was in the later Corded Ware that the heavy selection for these traits was effected \parencites{Wilde2014}{Ju2021}{Perretti2025}, traits which to this day reach their highest frequency in Northern Europe \parencite{Frost2006}.

\section{Old Germanic alliterative poetry}

  Having discussed the historical context of the Old Germanic peoples, it is time to turn to their poetry, the literature edited in this book.  As has already been alluded to, this unique form of verse, attested in all literary Germanic languages save Gothic, has strong stylistic and metrical commonalities across all its manifestations, so that we may truly speak of an Old Germanic poetic traditions.

  The historical and literary significance of the poetry may be thought of in two ways: first, as the beginning of native Germanic-language literature and second, as the final stage of an ancient oral tradition with distant Indo-European roots which long predates some of the subjects with which it dealt.

  To the first point, alliterative verse is the medium for the oldest indigenous Germanic-language literary monuments (the Gothic literature, chiefly consisting of Wulfila’s translation of the Bible, being wholly foreign in origin).  It is our earliest native source of knowledge about the Iron Age folk-life of Northern Europe and our most important source for the pre-Christian Germanic religion; it represents the worldview of the Germanic peoples in their earliest historical stages.  As the earliest recorded medium for the transmission of song and story amongst the ancestors of numerous great nations it has to some extent influenced all later Germanic languages and literatures.  The alliterative poetry, albeit eventually outcompeted by Latin, Greek, and Hebrew models, laid the ground for the German \emph{Minnessänger}, the \emph{Nibelungenlied}, and Chaucer, which in turn precede such figures as Goethe, Wagner, and Shakespeare.

  To the second point, however, this poetic genre is hardly a new art-form by the time we first encounter it in manuscript form in the 7th century \hyperref[Cadman]{Cadman’s Hymn}.  Its characteristic feature, alliteration between initial sounds, is attested in far older sources, and some of its poetic formulae seem to stretch as far back as the Bronze Age (see below).

  \subsection{Roots and origins}

  In the 1st century \ce, Tacitus (\emph{Germania} 2) speaks shortly of the ‘ancient songs’ in which the Germani documented their mythology and history.  He mentions the myth of three brothers, the sons of \emph{Mannus} ‘Man’, who founded three tribes, the \emph{Ingaevones, Herminones} and \emph{Istaevones}.  In their Proto-Germanic forms the names of the three tribes all begin with a vowel, and this may be called the earliest secure example of Germanic alliteration.  Other examples of three brothers with alliterating names are found in \Gutasaga\ 1 (\emph{Guti, Graipr, Gunn·fiaunn}) and in the three God-brothers Weden, Will, and Wigher (\textlink{Voluspa}[4] n.).

  Somewhat more tentatively, Tacitus may also give us the earliest example of the Germanic long-line, the same meter as \textlink{Beowulf} and the Poetic Edda, when he lists the following tribes as the worshippers of \emph{Nerthus}: \emph{Reudigni, Aviones, Anglii, Varini, Eudoses, Suardones, Nuithones} (\emph{Germania} 40).  If \emph{Nuithones} is emended to \emph{*Unithones}, which unlike \emph{Nuithones} actually looks like a Germanic word, the list may be arranged into two long-lines, both with vowel-alliteration \parencites{Malone1934}{Malone1936}:

  \begin{verse}
    \emph{Reudigni, \alst{A}viones, \hld\ \alst{A}nglii, Varini,} \\
    \emph{\alst{Eu}doses, Suardones, \hld\ *\alst{U}nithones.}
  \end{verse}

  A more secure example of a similar poetic list is found in the 2nd century, when Ptolemy lists the following tribes on the Cimbric peninsula: \emph{Saxones, Sigulones, Sabalingii, Cobandi, Chali, Phunusii, Charudes} (\emph{Geography} 2, 11:7).  This example has the advantage of not requiring any emendation, and forms two clean long-lines with normal alliterative patterns (a a : a x, a x : a [x]) \parencites{Malone1936}[179]{Neidorf2013}:

  \begin{verse}
    \emph{\alst{S}axones, \alst{S}igulones, \hld\ \alst{S}abalingii, Cobandi,} \\
    \emph{\alst{Ch}ali, Phunusii, \hld\ \alst{Ch}arudes.}
  \end{verse}

  The most likely explanation for how these two lists ended up in Classical texts is rather straight-forward: the Germanic informers recited the tribes in the order they had learnt them, namely as alliterative poems or rigamaroles not unlike those we find in later poems like \textlink{Widsith} or in the Icelandic poetic lists known as \emph{þulur} ‘thules’ \parencite{Malone1936}.

  The earliest truly indisputable native attestations of the long-line are in runic inscriptions from the early centuries \ce, the oldest of which is an early 3rd century sword-chape from Thorsberg moor in Schleswig.  These inscription are edited in the pres.~ed.~under Runic Poetry.
  In the runic finds we see not just the long-line in its perfected form, but also examples of stylistic traits common to all later Germanic alliterative poetry.  Thus, the 5th century Tjurkö bracteate (DR IK184) calls gold \emph{walha-kurné} (dat.~sg.) ‘Roman grain’, a kenning-like reference to the melting of Roman solidi; the 3rd century Thorsberg chape (DR 7) expresses the adjective ‘famous’ with the litotes \emph{ni wajé-máriʀ} ‘not ill-famed’ (cf.~\textlink{Atlakvida}[13]/1); and the 6th century Noleby stone (Vg 63) contains the formula \emph{rúnó ragina-kundó} (acc.~sg.) ‘rune born of the Gods’ later found in the 9th century \hyperref[Havamal:80]{\emph{Hǫ́va mǫ́l}, st.~80}.

  Of course, a far greater trove of rich expressions and images, many of great antiquity, is found in the extensive corpus of poetry written in the later mediaeval manuscripts.  In \Beowulf\ and the Edda, “sea-stallions” sail across the ocean like in Homer (\Odyssey\ 4.708);
  in \textlink{Grimnismal}[38], the Sun is drawn across Heaven in Her chariot like in \Rigveda\ and the Trundholm Sun Chariot (figg.~\ref{trundholm-front}--\ref{trundholm-back} pp.~\pageref{fig:trundholm-front}--\pageref{fig:trundholm-back});
  in \Beowulf, \textlink{Heliand}, \textlink{Atlakvida}, and various other poems, feasts are held in great chiefly “mead-halls”, places by no means unknown from the archaeology of the Nordic Bronze Age and the Migration Period.

  The richness of the Old Germanic poetic tradition is expressed through a complex language of kennings, stock phrases, and rare synonyms, many of which are found only in poetry.  Some of these synonyms are quite ancient, being old Indo-European words otherwise extinct in all Germanic languages which find their last refuge in poetry.
  Such are the Old English \emph{eoh}, Old Norse \emph{jór}, corresponding to the Sanskrit \textoi{áşva}, Latin \emph{equus}, all meaning ‘horse’; Old Norse \emph{týr}, corresponding to Sanskrit \textoi{dēvá}, Latin \emph{deus}, all meaning ‘god’; Old English \emph{folde}, Old Norse \emph{fold}, corresponding to Sanskrit \textoi{pr̥tʰivī́}, all meaning ‘land, (mother) earth’.  The fact that many of these words relate to the cult perhaps suggests that the Germanic religion was not as innovative as is commonly supposed.

  What a comparative investigation reveals is thus that these poetic motifs and words are seldom uniquely mediaeval, nor even inventions of the first centuries \ce; instead they often find parallels in texts and archaeology from as far back as the Bronze Age.  The poems themselves are clearly much younger than this, but still not so young that their undoubtedly unlatinate authors could have been influenced by texts like the Iliad, which were still yet unknown in Northern Europe; influence from \Rigveda\ is even more far-fetched.  Instead, they seem to retain ancient traditions which must have been passed down through an unbroken chain of oral poetry going back far into prehistory, back to the time when the Indo-Europeans were still nomadic steppe-pastoralists and much of Europe yet covered by thick forest and marshlands.

  \subsection{Structure}

    To describe the structure of Germanic alliterative poetry, it is first necessary to describe the general characteristics of the common Germanic alliterative meter as it is found in the earliest runic inscriptions, West Germanic poetry, and Norse Eddic poetry in the meter \Fornyrdislag.  The following description is by no means exhaustive and does not take into account the differences between the various traditions or some syntactical rules, but it does describe the bulk of the corpus.

    Alliterative poetry has two primary structural elements: \emph{stress} and \emph{alliteration}.  The exact count of syllables is generally less important, and end-rhyme is only used as a sporadic flourish.
    Based on alliteration, words are grouped into \emph{verses} and \emph{long-lines}.

    \subsubsection{Syllable weight}

    The smallest structural element is the \emph{syllable}.  In the poetry, a syllable may be either light or heavy, depending on the number of morae following its kernel.  It is of note that the syllable structure in poetry may not have been identical to the syllable structure of the Germanic language itself \parencite{Goering2023Prosody}.

    A long vowel (CVV) not followed by a consonant is counted as light and thereby equivalent to a short vowel followed by a single consonant (CVC).  A long vowel followed by a single consonant (CVVC) or a short vowel followed by two or more consonant are both counted as heavy.  Overheavy syllables with a long vowel and two or more consonants (CVVCC) are not treated any differently from heavy syllables.

    \subsubsection{Stress}

    Following the notation of \textlink{Suzuki2014}, each syllable is classified as having primary stress (p), secondary stress (s), or no stress (x).  Syllables marked p and s are stressed syllables, and those with x are unstressed syllables.  Word boundaries are noted with \#.

    Primary stress is reserved for the root syllable in a word, which is not always the same as the \emph{first} syllable.  Compare the English word “beginning”, where the primary stress falls on the syllable “ginn-”, yielding the stress pattern xPx.  It is important to note that not all words that can potentially carry stress are always stressed; the general rule is that infinite verbs and nominals are more likely to take primary stress than finite verbs.

    Secondary stress falls on the second element in a compound word, e.g., \emph{hęimr} in \emph{jǫtun-hęimr} ‘Ettin-ham’ (pxS).  In certain cases, it can also fall on the second word in a phrase, e.g., on \emph{fold} in \emph{søkkr fold} (P\#S) ‘Earth sinks’.

    No stress falls on inflectional syllables, pronouns, prepositions, or function words.  The exception for pronouns and prepositions is that they can take primary stress if it coincides with semantic stress, e.g., in a statement like “It was \emph{you}!”, and that they must take stress when they occur displaced from the first dip of a verse, e.g., in \Beowulf\ 19b: \emph{Scęde-landum in} (pxSx\#P) ‘in the Northern lands’.

    \subsubsection{Alliteration}

    The core structural element of the Old Germanic meter is \emph{alliteration} or the resonance between two stressed syllables beginning with the same phoneme, e.g., \emph{\alst{s}and} with \emph{re\alst{c}eive}, or \emph{\alst{g}reat} with \emph{be\alst{g}in}.  In the present edition, alliterating sounds are marked with red font.

    Alliterating stressed syllables are called \emph{staves}, and only stressed syllables can be staves; when an unstressed syllable happens to alliterate with a stave this is understood to be ornamental alliteration.

    Apart from the alliteration between consonants, the following rules obtain in all Germanic meters: any vowel or diphthong can alliterate with any other vowel or diphthong.  \emph{s} and the clusters \emph{sk, sp} and \emph{st} are counted as four distinct “sounds”.

    In Old English and Old Saxon poetry, \emph{g} and \emph{j} can alliterate with each other.  In Old Norse poetry, \emph{j} and \emph{v} can alliterate with vowels, the former always, the latter sporadically.

    \subsubsection{Positions}

    The smallest metrical division is the \emph{position}, a concept related (though not identical) to the syllable.  The position must be understood as broader than the syllable, since several syllables can fill one position, whereas a single syllable can never fill multiple positions.

    Positions are of two schematic types, the lift and the dip.  The lift can be either a full lift or a half-lift.  It is of note that “heavy dips” at times can serve as half- or even full lifts; likewise, secondarily stressed syllables can sometimes count as full lifts.  In order to properly distinguish positions from syllables, a full lift may be noted as /, a half-lift as \textbackslash, and a dip as x.

    A light stressed syllable followed by a light or heavy syllable can resolve into a single position, e.g., in \emph{jǫtun-hęimr} (pxS), which in most line-types is metrically identical to \emph{þrúð-hęimr} (pS).  Likewise, several unstressed syllables can count as a single dip.

    \subsubsection{Verses}

    Each \emph{verse} or \emph{half-line} is made up of 4 positions---typically 2 stressed, 2 unstressed---arranged according to set patterns or \emph{types}.  These types form the core of Sievers’ description of alliterative poetry, and the four-position system established by him is that followed throughout this edition.

    Sievers classifies verses into 5 generic types, named by descending order of frequency in Old English poetry.  They are given here according to their positional types; real examples may differ:

    Type A: / x / x

    Type B: x / x /

    Type C: x / / x

    Type D: / / \textbackslash x \emph{or} / / x \textbackslash

    Type E: / \textbackslash x / \emph{or} / x \textbackslash /

    As \textcite[240]{Goering2023} notes, these verses are epiphenomenal and arise on the basis of underlying metrical rules.  As mine is not a metrical work, however, I defer to his discussion.  For a list of subcategories of each type, see \textcite[240--242]{Goering2023Prosody}.

    \subsubsection{Lines}

    2 verses are linked together by shared alliteration across their staves to form a couplet or \emph{long-line} (often simply \emph{line}); these are the a-verse (or on-verse) and b-verse (or off-verse).  The two verses in each long-line are separated by a short pause or \emph{cæsura}.  With very rare exceptions, only the first lift in the b-verse may alliterate.  The a-verse may have one or two alliterating lifts, but if the second lift alliterates, the first must usually also do so.

    It is not uncommon for editions of Old Norse poetry to print each verse on its own line, e.g., \textlink{Voluspa}[2]/1--2:

    \begin{quote}\begin{small}
      \alst{E}k man \alst{jǫ}tna \\
      \alst{á}r of borna, \\
      þȧ’s \alst{f}orðum mik \\
      \alst{f}ǿdda hǫfðu;
    \end{small}\end{quote}

    Editions of West Germanic poetry typically print each long-line on its own line, with the cæsura designated by a space:

    \begin{quote}\begin{small}
      \alst{E}k man \alst{jǫ}tna \hspace*{1em} \alst{á}r of borna, \\
      þȧ’s \alst{f}orðum mik \hspace*{1em} \alst{f}ǿdda hǫfðu;
    \end{small}\end{quote}

    For consistency, the present edition follows the latter convention in the presentation of all alliterative poetry, Norse or West Germanic, but marks the cæsura with the interpunct:

    \begin{quote}\begin{small}
      \alst{E}k man \alst{jǫ}tna \hld\ \alst{á}r of borna, \\
      þȧ’s \alst{f}orðum mik \hld\ \alst{f}ǿdda hǫfðu;
    \end{small}\end{quote}

    \subsubsection{Fits and stanzas}

    The long-line is the highest metrical division, but long-lines can be combined into complete poems according to either a \emph{stichic} or \emph{stanzaic} structure.

    West Germanic poetry is typically \emph{stichic}.  This means that long-lines simply follow one other in a long chain, often with enjambment, so that a new sentence frequently begins in the b-verse.

    In longer epic poems, lines may be grouped into \emph{fits}, i.e., sections or cantos.  The fit does not have a fixed length, but is generally around 70--85 lines long.  The 3182-line \Beowulf\ is divided into 42 numbered fits for an average of 76 lines per fit; the surviving 5983 lines of \textlink{Heliand} are divided into 71 for an average of 84 lines per fit.

    (It may be noted that it is unlikely to be a coincidence that the length of the fit is similar to the length of shorter poems like \textlink{GudrunThree}, \textlink{Finnsburg}, or \textlink{Hildebrandslied}; there was likely a longer pause between each fit, which may each have been seen as its own “song” or “chapter” in the longer poems.)

    In \textlink{Heliand} a new fit can begin on a b-verse.  This does not happen in \Beowulf.

    North Germanic poetry is always more or less \emph{stanzaic} and does not have fits.  Each \emph{stanza} consists of several long-lines, and enjambment is rare.  The regularity of the stanzas varies by poem and meter, but there is a general trend towards stanzas of exactly 4 lines.

    In Norse 4-line stanzas, there is generally a strong semantic division between lines 2 and 3, often signalled by the use of a conjunction like \emph{ok} ‘and’ or \emph{ęn} ‘but’ as the first word in line 3.  In the native Norse poetic tradition, each group of 2 lines is said to form a \emph{helming} or \emph{half-stanza}.


\section{The corpus}

  The scope of the present edition is large, and, when complete, will encompass much of the alliterative poetry extant in Old Germanic languages.  The poetry is divided into the following categories:

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Norse Mythic poetry}, i.e., that which directly treats the Germanic mythology.  This category is exclusively Norse for the simple reason that no West Germanic or Gothic mythic narrative poetry survive.
    \item \textbf{Norse Heroic poetry}, specifically the whole second half of the Codex Regius and a few other works; with a few exceptions, subject matter outside of the Walsing cycle is not included.
    \item \textbf{West Germanic Heroic Poetry} in Old English, Old Saxon, and Old High German.
    \item \textbf{Poetry on Christian subjects}.  This category includes explicitly Christian poems where the new religion or its mythology is at the core of the work.  Christian heroic poems depicting native legends, like \Beowulf\ and \textlink{Hildebrandslied}, are not included.
    \item \textbf{Galders}, i.e., alliterative spells and charms, both from runic inscriptions and mediaeval manuscripts.
    \item \textbf{Runic Poetry}, apart from that already edited under Galders above.
  \end{enumerate}

  \subsection{Exclusions}
    All Norse Scaldic poetry is excluded, as is the Eddic poetry found in the Saws of Icelanders and Saws of Antiquity (\emph{forn-aldar sǫgur}).  Three reasons justify this exclusion.  First, these two categories of poetry have already been edited by the \Skp\ series, in the case of the Scaldic poetry admirably so; second, the underlying poetry is often impossible to take out of its prose context; and third, it is doubtful whether most of the Eddic poetry embedded in prose contexts ever had a life of its own, or whether it was composed specifically to accompany the prose.  For these reasons, I would opt only to edit the poetry in its original context, that is, edit the whole Saws, and that is an undertaking that falls too far outside of the scope of the present edition.

    Most Old English poems that do not deal with legendary material are excluded.  This includes poems on historical subjects embedded in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, the Riddles of the Exeter Book, the Biblical epics, and the elegies like “The Seafarer” and “The Wanderer”.

  \subsection{Manuscripts}

    The source manuscripts employed for the edition will be discussed at greater length in the introduction to each section, but since some languages are scattered across several sections, it may be useful to discuss their manuscript situations together.

    \subsubsection{Old Norse poetry}

    The Old Norse poetry primarily derives from the Codex Regius of the Prose Edda (\Regius).  This, and other lesser manuscripts, will be discussed in the Introduction to Norse Mythic Poetry.

    \subsubsection{Old English poetry}

    The edited Old English poetry primarily derives from a few manuscripts.  Particularly important are the Exeter Book (\ExeterBook) and \Lacnunga.

    \subsubsection{Old Saxon and High German poetry}

    There are no collections or anthologies of alliterative poetry in either of these languages; instead the manuscript situation will be discussed in the introduction to each individual text.

\section{This edition}

  The present edition is divided into three main parts, presented side by side: the edition, the translation, and the commentary.  Every stanza or small group of roughly 3--8 lines is presented edited in the original language, and immediately below in a literal English translation.  In the right margin, a list of manuscript witnesses is given for the particular passage.  Below the English translation, there follow two layers of notes.  The first contains critical notes to the edition based on the manuscripts, and the second contains all other notes and commentary.  Broader commentary or critical notes dealing with the entire passage are labelled ALL, whereas more specific notes are linked to specific lines, phrases, or words.

  \subsection{The original-language edition}

    The present edition encompasses texts in four Old Germanic languages: Old Norse, Old English, Old Saxon, and Old High German.  All texts have been normalised according to a standardised orthography for the respective language.  The various orthographies are designed to follow three core principles:

    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Faithfulness to the spoken language at the time when the texts were written, and the distinctions demonstrably found therein;
      \item Respect for the etymological origin of words, and their distinctions;
      \item Striving for a uniform orthography across the various languages, so that the same sound, where possible, should be written with the same character.
    \end{enumerate}

    These choices often come into conflict with the orthography of the original manuscripts and with earlier philological tradition; for this reason there is some need to justify them.  My goal is to render the texts themselves in a manner that gives as much philological information to the reader as possible---not to present a facsimile edition.  This also enables easier pronunciation and metrical scansion of the poetry, which is one of the goals of the edition as a whole.

    In any case, this decision follows the philological methods used for printing, e.g., the \Rigveda, which is generally reproduced in an entirely scholarly latinized orthography, not in the original \textoi{Devanāgarī}.
    Regardless, such important traits of the original manuscript tradition as the long \emph{ſ}, arbitrary punctuation, arbitrary spelling, and lack of line breaks, are seldom reproduced in any modern editions of Old Germanic poetry.

    \subsubsection{General orthographic conventions}

    The following orthographic conventions are applied for all Old Germanic languages:

    \begin{enumerate}
    \item The voiceless dental fricative is always written with the letter \emph{þ}, never \emph{th}.
    \item Long vowels are marked with the acute accent, never the macron or circumflex,
    \item excepting those long vowels that have their origin in earlier diphthongs; they are written with the circumflex.
    \item In compounds where the first element has primary stress, the elements are separated with a dash (-),
    \item but where the first element is a prefix that cannot stand on its own, they are separated with an interpunct (·).
    \item Productive non-inflectional suffixes are marked with a double oblique hyphen (⸗).
    \end{enumerate}

    The following specifications apply to the specific languages.

    \subsubsection{Normalisation of Old Norse}

    My Old Norse orthography is inspired by \textcite{FinnurEdda} in that it strives for a more archaic form than that of the surviving mss., viz., a form that represents the language of the poetry at the most likely time of composition.  For this reason, the orthography often has more in common with the proposed orthography of the First Grammatical Treatise \parencite{FGTHaugen} than with the standard Old Icelandic orthography seen in most editions.  The following list describes the differences from the standard Old Icelandic orthography seen in modern editions:

    \begin{enumerate}
    \item I distinguish between short \emph{e} (from etymological short \emph{e}) and short \emph{ę} (from etymological short \emph{a} + \emph{i}-umlaut).
    \item I distinguish between long \emph{á} and \emph{ǫ́}, as done by the First Grammatical Treatise.
    \item I use \emph{ǿ} and \emph{ę́} (rather than the usual \emph{œ} and \emph{æ}) to represent the vowels descended from Proto-Norse \emph{ó} and \emph{á} after \emph{i}-umlaut (cf.~the short \emph{ø, ę} < \emph{o, a} + \emph{i}-umlaut).
    \item I distinguish between long nasal \emph{ȧ, ė, i̇́, ȯ, u̇} and long oral \emph{á, é, í, ó, ú}, as done in the First Grammatical Treatise.
    \item I restore the old \emph{s} (which in modern Scandinavian and even in most Old Norse manuscripts has become \emph{r}, but which is found in old manuscripts such as AM 237 a fol (ca.~1150) and is fossilised in forms like \emph{þaz} (= \emph{þat’s}) in \Regius) in the words \emph{er} < \emph{es} ‘which, that, where, when’, and in inflections of \emph{vera} < \emph{vesa} such as \emph{er} < \emph{es} ‘is’ (3rd sg.~pres.~ind.) and \emph{var} < \emph{vas} (3rd sg.~pret.~ind.).
    \item Note: The following forms of \emph{vesa} retain the \emph{r}, as it is there the result of Verner’s law, and not of this much younger sound change: the pl.~pres.~ind.~(\emph{erum} etc.), the pl.~pret.~ind.~(\emph{vǫ́rum} etc.), and the pl.~pret.~subj.~(\emph{vę́rim} etc.).
    \item When metrically benefactory, I contract \emph{ek} ‘I’, \emph{eru} ‘are’, and \emph{es} ‘which; is’ to \emph{’k}, \emph{’ro} and \emph{’s}, respectively.
    \item Note: I follow \textcite{FinnurEdda} in distinguishing between the contractions of the relative particle \emph{es} and the verb \emph{es}: the particle is appended to the previous word with only an apostrophe (e.g., \emph{hann’s} ‘he who’), while the verb is separated by a space (e.g., \emph{hann ’s} ‘he is’).
    \item Where metrically cumbersome and likely the result of younger scribal tampering, unstressed personal pronouns are silently removed (cf.~Scaldic poetry, \textlink{Volundarkvida}[21]/1--2 vs. 23/3--4, \textlink{HelgakvidaOne}[32] vs. \textlink{HelgakvidaTwo}[18])
    \item I resolve \emph{z} to \emph{ts, ds, þs} or \emph{ðs} depending on its underlying elements.
    \item \emph{x} is, however, still used instead of \emph{ks}.
    \end{enumerate}

    \subsubsection{Normalisation of Old Swedish and Danish}
    For these dialects, I generally employ the same conventions as those for Old West Norse, including the marking of \emph{u}-mutated \emph{a} > \emph{ǫ}.  That this change indeed occurred also in the Eastern Nordic dialects is easily proven by the third-person personal pronoun, which shows \emph{u}-mutation in such forms as Swedish \emph{honom} ‘him’ < \emph{hǫ̇num}, \emph{hon} ‘she’ < \emph{hǫ̇n}).

    A few changes are specific to OSwe and ODan.  According to rule 3 in the general orthographic conventions above, I distinguish between \emph{ǿ} (< \emph{ǿ}) from \emph{ø̂} (< \emph{au, ęy}) and \emph{é} (< \emph{é}) from \emph{ê} (< \emph{ęi}).

    Where unstressed vowels have been reduced to a schwa-like sound, this is written with \emph{e}.

    \subsubsection{Normalisation of Old English}

    I write fronted or brightened etymological \emph{a} and \emph{á} with \emph{æ} and \emph{ǽ}, for instance in \emph{dæg} ‘day’ (< \emph{*dagaʀ}) and \emph{rǽd} ‘advice, counsel’ (< \emph{rádaʀ}).  These are contrasted with \emph{ę} and \emph{ę́}, which represent \emph{i}-mutated \emph{a} and \emph{á}, e.g., in \emph{ęllæn} ‘zeal, courage’ (< \emph{*aljaną}).

    An assimilated \emph{n} is marked with an overpoint, like in rule 3 of Old Norse above.

  \subsection{The English translation and commentary}

    It is worth it to discuss my translation philosophy and the point and purpose of the translation and commentary at some length.

    \subsection{A new translation}

    There is by now a large number of translations of the most popular alliterative poetic texts, namely \Beowulf\ and the \emph{Poetic Edda}.  These generally fall into two camps:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \emph{poetic} translations, which distort the precise meaning of the text for the sake of meter, often quite radically; and
      \item \emph{prose} translations, which nowise preserve the style or feeling of the original.
    \end{enumerate}

    Almost all translations, regardless of camp, also tend to show the following inadequacies: obscuring or glossing over difficult technical and cultural terminology; rendering identically repeated phrases and words (formulae) differently at various places; and simplifying or rewriting kennings and other poetic expressions.  Even worse, this is often done with little in the way of notes or commentary, to a point where the reader is sometimes left entirely oblivious to the sense of the original text.

    What sets my translation apart from previous English translations is that it aims to follow the style and register of the original text closely, without sacrificing the literal sense of the words, e.g., by removing or inserting matter where it is not present in the original.  The downside of this is that literality and consistency at times must come at the cost of fluent and idiomatic English, but it has the advantage of giving the reader an idea not just of \emph{what} the original text says, but \emph{how} it says it.  In many cases, we can only understand the former by first knowing the latter.

    Maybe this over-literality is a pointless effort?  One could say that a translation is always a betrayal, and that those truly interested in the precise sense of the original text should study it in its original language.  While I do agree that the sufficiently interested reader should learn the languages in which these texts were written (a task in which he would be much helped by the present parallel edition, with its clear orthography and extensive notes), it is still a “hard ask” for those readers who are not philologically or linguistically inclined, but instead students and scholars of history, comparative mythology, religion, anthropology, or literature---those who, for whatever reason, are interested in exploring the oldest poetic heritage of the Germanic peoples of Northern Europe.

    It is my hope that such people will find the philological and religious musings in the notes interesting and enlightening, and not be scared off by the translation style or the breadth of the noets.  The reader should keep in mind that he is venturing into a foreign land.  To paraphrase Robert Alter, there ought to be more to English prose than \emph{The New York Times} and \emph{The Guardian}.

    \subsection{The proper nouns}

      The single most idiosyncratic part of the present translation will undoubtedly be its handling of proper nouns.  I have opted to render cultural and religious terms, names of places, heroes, gods, and other entities by their natural English cognates (e.g., \emph{Thunder} for Old Norse \emph{Þórr}); or, where such do not exist, by their philologically expected English or “Anglish” forms (e.g., \emph{*wallow} for Old Norse \emph{vǫlva}).  Due to the parallel original-language edition and index, it is hoped that no vital information will be lost by this decision; the reader wishing to know the original form of Webthrithner (\emph{Vaf·þrúðnir}), e.g., can either look in the parallel Old Norse, or, if the case endings confuse him, peruse the index.

      There are three reasons behind this choice.  The first is ideological.  It is my belief that the Old Germanic myths and poems and gods and heroes are a shared Northern European heritage.  When one compares these mediaeval texts from Germany, England, and Scandinavia, one cannot but notice the striking similarity of their diction, the reoccurence of figures and formulae, and the common worldview.  Likewise, the personages are frequently the same: the Scandinavian hero \emph{Vǫlundr} is the same character as the English \emph{Wélund}, the Norse god \emph{Óðinn} is the same as the English \emph{Wóden}, and this is how they were understood by the native speakers of the old tongues.  Since English is a Germanic language and its speakers the linguistic heirs of the Anglo-Saxons and Norse---in many case their direct descendants---and since these varying names are ultimately mere distinctions in pronunciation which reflect shared underlying forms, it was felt important and valuable to render them in the English translation by their natural or expected English reflexes.  The forms of the names thereby serve to reflect the continuity between the speakers of modern English and those of the Old Germanic languages.

      The second reason is one of consistency.  If we are to restore well-attested names like Weden and Thunder, it would not be consistent to retain others in an unadapted form (e.g., to have Weden conversing with \emph{Vaf·þrúðnir}, or Thunder with \emph{Hár·barðr}), which would produce a highly discordant effect.  Since the Anglish names are to be restored in some places, consistency requires that they be restored in all, even where they do not survive in modern English (or even in Old English).

      The third reason is one of linguistic aesthetics.  It is an indisputable fact that the commonly accepted forms like \emph{Odin}, \emph{Thor}, \emph{Midgard} etc.~are debased.  They do not even represent the Old Norse pronunciation as accurately as would be possible within the constraints of English ortography (e.g., \emph{Odin} would be better anglicized as \emph{Othin}).  What is more, they are often difficult for English speakers to pronounce and lead to absurd errors and mispronunciations.  The fluent reader of Old Norse cannot but shudder when he hears \emph{ę́sir} and \emph{Ǫ́s·garðr} pronounced as \textipa{/aI"sI:\*r/} and “ass-guard”, or when he sees \emph{Jǫrmun·gandr} spelled “Jormangander”.  The native English forms, on the other hand, fit well within English orthography, and their pronunciation is immediately recognisable.

    \subsection{Commentary}

      A final note.  As I already touched on above, the goal of the commentary is to enlighten and stimulate the reader in his self-guided study of the edited texts.  In order to properly understand these poems, we ought to see them both from the view of literary texts and as antiquarian sources for the ancient worldview and religion.  If we forget to see them as literature, we risk losing the most important part of the poetry to its audience---its music.  If we neglect their historical context, however, we will be like a child in a concert-hall, sitting idly and ignorant of the great art to which it is bearing witness.

      Although frequently extensive, the commentary does not aim to be wordy for its own sake.  Through the cross-references, philological discussions, and illustrated archaeological parallels, it is hoped that the reader may come to a deeper understanding of the Old Germanic world-view and religion.  By exploring its parallels, not just in Northern Europe, but further afield, we can all discover dimensions in the text that have previously lain hidden.  The reader who grasps the cultural and spiritual significance of the ancient worldview will himself become a custodian of a rich and woefully understudied Northern European heritage, the Northern Epics.
